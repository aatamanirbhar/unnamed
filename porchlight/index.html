Porchlight

— ONE HALLOWEEN. SIX DOORS —
The street is the wrong kind of quiet for a Tuesday.

Down at the cul-de-sac, somebody's porch is still playing the same album they were playing at five o'clock — the orange-cover Carpenters, Top of the World looping from a single open window. The Andersons. Their daughter went to college in Tampa and the house still listens to her playlist out of habit.

Your pillowcase is two-thirds full. Snickers, Reese's, three Whoppers, one apple from the Vermillions which you'll throw out when nobody's looking. The pumpkin you've been carrying since the first porch is almost out of candle.

Five porches left on your loop. Then home. Then bed. Then this might be the year you stopped.

A short story in six knocks. ~15 minutes. Choices matter, a little.

You cross the street at the diagonal, like a kid who's been doing this loop since you were five.
A jack-o-lantern on the Petersons' porch has collapsed into itself. The smell is sweet and slightly burnt.

Up ahead: the Gordons.
— PORCH ONE —
The Gordons4408 Linden Court

The Gordons go big every year. The lawn is a graveyard. There's a fog machine pouring milk-thick air into a Tupperware bin of dry ice. An animatronic Frankenstein lurches every six seconds whether you trip the sensor or not — the sensor's been broken since 2019 — and Mr. Gordon, dressed as a vampire in a Members Only jacket, sits at a card table with a metal tray and a smile that's working too hard.

"Last loop?" he says. "I figured. You're up late for a Tuesday."

The tray has Snickers, Twix, Reese's, full-size. Two of each left. He has been giving these away since 5:30 and he has, you suspect, been waiting for you.

He watches you walk off. You can feel it on the back of your neck the way you feel a dog watch you out a window.

The Frankenstein goes off again, six seconds on the dot, into the empty yard.

You wonder, briefly, how many candy bars are left now.

Past the Petersons. Past the house where the new family hasn't been here long enough to decorate. Past the cottonwood that drops its leaves all at once around the end of October — half of them are on the sidewalk and they crackle like newspaper.

The next porch has its light on but the door doesn't.
— PORCH TWO —
Take One4422 Linden Court · light on, door closed

A folding chair holds down a plastic skull bowl. TAKE ONE in a Sharpie hand on a square of cardboard, the lettering of an adult who pretends to be bad at lettering. Four mini Hershey's left. A flat seven-pack of Smarties balanced on top, which somebody else clearly brought to share into rather than take from. The wreath on the door is woven corn husks.

The porch light has the slight buzz of a bulb thinking about going. Nobody is watching.

Inside, very faintly, a kitchen radio. A laugh, far off, like from another room of the house.

You back down the steps quietly, like you might if there was a baby asleep upstairs.

At the bottom of the walk you look back. The bowl is the way you left it. The porch light still buzzes. The wind moves the corn husks on the wreath and one leaf comes loose.

Two more streetlights. The Carpenters are still going somewhere behind you. You realize you've been hearing them this whole time and only just noticed again — that loop of Top of the World, looping, looping.

You stop in front of a house you've stopped in front of every Halloween of your life.
— PORCH THREE —
The Screen Door4509 Linden Court · the Ferguson house

The bell doesn't work. It hasn't worked in seven years. You knock on the screen door and the screen rattles against the frame in the way it always has.

From inside: the sound of slippers. The slow kind. The inside door opens first, the screen second, and a small old man stands there in the gold light of the entryway in a brown cardigan.

"Halloween," he says. Not as a question.

"Yes sir."

He looks at your costume — a 1990s detective, beige overcoat, a thrift-store tie, a fake fedora that's a little too big. He takes that in like it's an answer to a different question. Then he says, "Wait there."

You wait. The entryway smells like cinnamon and old paper.

He comes back with a wax-paper bag. Homemade caramels. The kind nobody's allowed to take anymore.

He nods once. He shuts the screen door first, the inside door second. Through the curtain you see him stop in the hallway, hand on the wall, like he forgot where he was going.

The porch is suddenly very dark.

You tell him about your mom. About the rule. About how it's not personal.

He nods once. He understands. He understood, you suspect, before you finished the first sentence.

"Smart mother," he says. "You go on now."

He shuts the screen door first, the inside door second. Through the curtain you see him still standing in the hallway after the doors are closed, the wax-paper bag in his hand, looking at it.

"What's your name?" you ask, on the porch, because you don't know why but it feels wrong to leave without asking.

He looks at you for a long second.

"Russ," he says. "Russ Ferguson. My wife used to make these. She'd've been ninety-two next month."

The caramels in the bag are still warm.

He doesn't say anything else for a moment. Then: "You're a good kid. Go on. The little ones will be in bed soon. You should be too."

He shuts the screen door, gently. Then the inside door. The porch light goes out a moment later. You stand there with a wax-paper bag of caramels you have decided, on the way down the steps, that you are going to eat.

The wind shifts. It's colder now. You can see your breath, just barely, when you walk under the streetlight by the stop sign.

The next house should have its porch on. It doesn't.
— PORCH FOUR —
No Pumpkin4517 Linden Court · the Kowalski house

No pumpkin. No light. No bowl. But somebody opened the gate this morning — the chain is on the inside of the fence, lying in the leaves.

You know this house. Mrs. Kowalski. Polish flag in the window for as long as you've been a kid. She always did peanut M&Ms and a riddle.

The flag isn't in the window.

You think about last week, when there was an ambulance two streets over and you didn't think to ask whose. You think about it for a long moment, on the dark walkway, with your half-full pillowcase getting heavier.

You knock. Three times, like a polite kid.

Nothing. Not the kind of nothing where someone might be home. The other kind. You stand on the porch for an extra second because it feels wrong to leave too fast. The wind moves the leaves on the mat. There's a faint outline on the wood where the welcome mat used to be lined up straight and isn't anymore.

You go back down the steps slowly. At the bottom you stop and look up at the front window — out of habit, expecting to be waved at — and there isn't anyone there. There isn't anyone there in a way that feels permanent.

You unwrap a Reese's from your bag — no, you put it down still wrapped, that's right — and set it on the welcome mat, off to the side where it won't get stepped on.

You're not sure why. She's not coming back tonight. She might not be coming back at all. But you do it anyway, and on the way down the steps you find you're walking slower, the way you walk leaving a church.

You keep your eyes on the sidewalk and walk past it like you would a stranger making a phone call. You feel the windows on your shoulders even so. You don't turn your head.

At the property line you allow yourself one quick look back. The flag is not in the window. The porch is dark. The leaves on the walk are exactly the way they were.

You will think about this porch later, in bed, and you will not be sure why.

You turn the corner. The Murthys' is on the next block, and even from here you can see their fog machine going. It throws a halo of low cloud across the lawn that the streetlight pushes through in yellow shafts.

Up the Murthys' driveway, another trick-or-treater is walking. They're wearing exactly your costume.
— PORCH FIVE —
The Other Detective4601 Linden Court · the Murthys

Tan coat. Thrift-store tie. Fake fedora a little too big. They've even got the plastic badge from the same Spirit aisle you got yours from.

You both stop walking. The Murthys' fog machine wheezes between you. For a second the only sound is the dry-ice hiss and the song that's still going somewhere far behind you — Top of the World — and the moment is so long it gets its own weather.

"Hey," the other detective says, in a voice that's a little too old for the costume.

They laugh — a startled, grateful laugh, like they hadn't been sure how this was going to go.

"My dad's idea," they say. "He's a cop. Was a cop. Whatever."

You walk up the driveway together. Mrs. Murthy opens the door already laughing. "Oh my GOD," she says, "the boys from L.A. Confidential. Vikram come look at this."

You both get a full-size Twix. The other detective fist-bumps you at the bottom of the steps and goes the other way down the cul-de-sac, the way you'd send a colleague off after a long shift.

You will not see them again on this street. You will think about them every Halloween for ten years.

You nod, the way an adult nods at another adult on a hiking trail. You walk past them. You feel them not say anything back.

Mrs. Murthy gives you a Twix, smaller than the one she'd give the kid behind you, you can already tell. "That's a nice costume, honey," she says, and she means it, but her eyes are already on the porch behind you, on the other coat coming up the driveway.

You think about turning back, just to say something. You don't.

You take off. So do they. The fog parts in the shape of two coats running. You get to the porch first because you knew the loose flagstone was there and they didn't — your elbow catches them in the ribs on the second step and you both laugh in the surprised way that has hurt in it.

Mrs. Murthy gives you both Twix. The same size. She does not laugh.

The other detective doesn't fist-bump you at the bottom of the steps. They walk off down the cul-de-sac the other way, more slowly than they were walking before.

The block ends at a stop sign and your house is the second on the right. The porch light is on. Your mom's pumpkin in the window is a tilted-triangle smile, candle still going.

You are almost home.
— PORCH SIX —
Home4622 Linden Court · your own porch

The lights are on. The pumpkin in the window your mom carved is a tilted triangle smile, candle still going. On your porch she's set out a folding chair, a wicker bowl, and a sign on copy paper in her handwriting:

KIDS ONLY · TAKE TWO
we're still up if you need a bandaid

The bowl has been picked clean. Two Almond Joys and a roll of Smarties at the bottom. Behind the door you can hear the TV — something with a laugh track.

Your sister is eight. She has had a fever since Friday. She has been the inside of the house all night with your mom while you walked.
